Index calculus is the method that should exploit the most structure in an elliptic-curve discrete-logarithm instance, and in practice it has never beaten Pollard rho on a prime-order curve of cryptographic shape. This paper surveys where that question stands and measures it. We review the literature by field type - extension fields (Gaudry, Diem, FGHR), prime-degree binary fields and the contested first-fall-degree heuristics behind the subexponential claims (Petit-Quisquater, Semaev, and the evidence of Huang, Kosters and Yeo, Shantz-Teske and Galbraith-Gebregiyorgis), subfield curves (Galbraith-Granger-Merz-Petit) and prime fields (Petit-Kosters-Messeng, Amadori-Pintore-Sala, McGuire-Mueller) - and the generic lower bounds that frame them. We then measure the frontier with one native harness that charges rho, baby-step giant-step, the kangaroo and several index-calculus pipelines in a single counted unit on one-target workloads with sealed, replayable records. Almost no cost in such records is complete, the rho baselines included, so we price every arm's unpriced native work alike across a band of weights and rank two methods only where the order holds across the band. On fixed ladders of eight Koblitz curves (log2 r from 11 to 39), six NIST-shaped prime curves (20-40 bits) and five Kummer-tower curves - 4,848 executions on public targets, parameters chosen on separate training targets, every session replay-audited - no index-calculus arm beats generic rho on any curve at any weight. The closest, a compact Frobenius-orbit factor base, is 3.8-15x signed-Frobenius rho with native work free; two-summand prime-field index calculus goes from 10.5x to 102x rho between 20 and 40 bits; no fitted cost exponent of a campaign index-calculus arm lies below rho's. The measurements come from a sixteen-week engineering programme that we also document: root-finding decomposition oracles (a known mechanism; its root identity, Proposition 1, is proved in Lean, and the oracle is engineered to 383-1,535x over matrix F4 per decomposition), an F_p F4 brought to msolve parity on square systems, FGHR's D_4 symmetry and its weighted and Frobenius-reduced forms cutting one k=4 decomposition by 48x and then 370-481x, factor bases closed under known-logarithm maps (Z[tau], CM, integer and cofactor multipliers) that cut cost 1.3-20x at an unchanged exponent, and three weak families at or near rho: genus-three index calculus after the cubic GHS transfer (about q^1.4 against q^1.5; below the strongest rho at q=2^16 under a preregistered rule and pricing, unresolved at other sizes to 2^20, above it under a heavier sensitivity pricing) and k=4 index calculus over F_{(2^m)^4}, whose gap to rho closes from 5,080x to 6.1x between n~2^24 and 2^56 at Gaudry's n^(-1/4), with a crossover extrapolated near 2^68, and a Joux-Vitse genus-three cover of E(F_{p^6}) (plain Nagao arm), at 1.6-2.0x a pooled rho constant at p=1009 and closing as about p^(-2) (a lower-bound comparison). We also record the negative results, including a conservation lemma for isogeny transfers and an equal-advice audit that retires the programme's online wins over rho: Bernstein-Lange preprocessing rho with the same table is 53-317x faster, and at n=83 it wins every pair run with a table matched on set-up time or memory. Separate sections cover Koblitz, random and prime-degree binary, and prime-field curves, and an appendix catalogues all 1,444 studies, benchmarks, problem records and notes of the programme by family, with each one's headline number and status. No result here is yet compared with a published index calculus at the same parameters; we state that reference arm, with the other open problems, as the experiment or proof that would settle it. Nothing here concerns a deployed curve or key recovery.
