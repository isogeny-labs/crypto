P-224 Auxiliary Curves

What an "auxiliary curve" is, and why it isn't a hidden twin of P-224

Cheon's attack, the curves this repository calls "auxiliary", and P-224's isogenous neighbours are three different things. This page draws each one and shows where P-224's actual numbers enter.

Short answer

  • An auxiliary curve is scaffolding you build yourself. It is picked for a convenient group order or a convenient set of x-coordinates. It is not secretly attached to P-224 and carries no hidden information about the key.
  • It is not an isogenous curve ("isogeny", not "misogyny"). P-224's isogenous curves have exactly the same number of points, n. They are relatives, not helpers. This repo walked 20,000 of them and found no change in discrete-log cost.
  • Cheon's attack needs extra input: the protocol must leak [xd]G as well as [x]G. With that leak, the attack works in the world of the secret scalar x. That world is Z/n, and the numbers that matter there are n − 1 and n + 1. P-224's famous smooth p − 1 plays no part in Cheon's attack.

1. Four worlds, and what travels between them

Almost all the confusion comes from mixing up these four worlds. P-224's points have coordinates in Fp. The secret key x is a number mod n, the group order. An auxiliary curve lives over one of those two fields, chosen by whoever runs the attack.

Solid boxes are fixed by the standard. Dashed boxes are objects an attacker chooses. Grey: P-224's own isogeny class. Factorisations of n ± 1 were computed natively for this page (trial division and Pollard–Brent; q₁₉₅ and q₁₇₇ are probable primes of 195 and 177 bits).

2. How Cheon's attack actually splits x

You are given G, [x]G and [xd]G, where d divides n − 1. Write the unknown as x = ζk. Then xd lives in a much smaller subgroup of Fn*, of size (n − 1)/d. You can test a guess k₁ by multiplying the leaked point by a known number, ζ−d·k₁, and comparing points. This is ordinary scalar multiplication on P-224, so it is legal. Baby-step giant-step therefore finds k mod (n − 1)/d. A second, smaller search with [x]G finds the rest.

3. What it would cost on P-224 (derived, not run)

These are log₂ group-operation counts from the textbook formulas, using the exact factorisations of n ± 1 above. Every Cheon row needs the protocol to hand over many powers of x. A plain ECDSA or ECDH key leaks none, so for it only the rho row applies.

Formulas: rho √(πn/4). Cheon (n − 1): √(n/d) + √d with d = 2^29.0. Cheon (n + 1): √(n/d) + d with d = 2·7·19·641707280681 ≈ 2^47.3, using powers up to x^{2d}. A static Diffie–Hellman server (one that multiplies any point you send by its fixed key) gives one power per query; this is the Brown–Gallant setting. These are derived costs for P-224, not runs. The repo's measured Cheon runs (toy sizes, research/torsion_auxiliary_inputs_20260918/) put the n − 1 version at about 20× its floor, beating rho from 24 bits up.

4. Where an auxiliary curve would fit, and why it stalls

Cheon is stuck with divisors of n ± 1, because Fn* and the norm-one torus of Fn² have sizes n − 1 and n + 1. The auxiliary-curve idea is to swap in an elliptic curve E′ over Fn whose number of points has a divisor of your choice. That would free d from n ± 1. The obstacle is mechanical. Walking on E′ means adding points, and adding points needs division. Here the "numbers" are hidden in exponents (you hold [x]G, not x), and you cannot divide inside an exponent with a few queries.

The same trick does work with a full Diffie–Hellman oracle. Maurer and Wolf use an auxiliary curve over F_n with smooth order to show that discrete log reduces to Diffie–Hellman. That is a statement about how hard the two problems are relative to each other, not an attack on keys. Source: research/notes/ecdlp-general/RESEARCH_TORSION_AUXILIARY_INPUTS.md.

5. The other "auxiliary curve": PKM's factor base over F_p

Index calculus on P-224 needs a set of "small" points. Petit, Kosters and Messeng define one by its x-coordinates. Take a set V ⊂ Fp built from a tower of low-degree maps. In one version, the maps are a chain of 2-isogenies on an auxiliary curve E′ over the same field Fp. This is the one place isogenies really appear, but they act on E′, not on P-224. E′ only supplies the shape of V, which keeps the decomposition equations at low degree. For P-224 you do not even need E′. Because p − 1 = 2⁹⁶·(odd), the roots of unity μ2t give the same tower shape directly, with t up to 96. That is why P-224's smooth p − 1 shows up in PKM discussions and not in Cheon's.

So, is it a curve that "gives excess structure"?

Only in the sense that a ruler gives you structure. It is a tool the attacker brings. It cannot add information that isn't already in the inputs. In Cheon's attack the extra information is the leaked powers of x, and the auxiliary group only lets you use them efficiently. With only G and [x]G, as for an ordinary P-224 key, no auxiliary curve in this repository's measurements gets below rho.
